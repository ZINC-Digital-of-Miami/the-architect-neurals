The lede

On August 14 the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency granted preliminary conditional approval for World Liberty Trust Company, N.A. — a national trust bank whose president and director is Zachary Witkoff, whose product is the family's USD1 stablecoin, and whose indirect investors, named in the OCC's own decision document, include DT Marks SC LLC, the Trump-family investment vehicle. The federal chartering record itself now documents the structure: the president's family holds a passive stake in a federally chartered bank that issues the dollar-pegged token whose sales route 75% of proceeds to the family, while the Treasury Department that supervises that regulator has gone eight and a half months without answering a Senate letter asking who bought the token. A OCC Corporate Decision #1385, Control No. 2026-Charter-344521.

The same week, the primary records dismantled three pieces of the story as this corpus had been carrying it. That is the second half of the lede, because a brief that only adds and never subtracts isn't applying its own standard.

Architecture I — The family money

The bank. The OCC decision (Aug 14) approves World Liberty Trust Company, N.A. of Bay Harbor Islands, Florida, to issue and redeem USD1 and provide digital-asset custody as fiduciary. Zachary Witkoff is organizer, director, and President; Robert Witkoff and Scott Alper are organizers/directors. The approval carries conditions — GENIUS Act compliance or divestiture, $20M minimum Tier 1 capital, prior OCC approval of executives — and, critically, passivity commitments from three indirect investors: DT Marks SC LLC, StringZ Holding RSC (DE) LLC, and AMGUS, LLC, each barred from board seats or control. Seven public comments raising Trump-family conflicts, UAE investor exposure, and BSA/AML concerns were rejected as outside charter-review scope or addressed. A Sen. Warren, on the record: “If the application is approved, you would promulgate rules that influence the profitability of the President's company.” USD1 sits near $4B B.

The structural description needs no embellishment: the same family name appears on the token issuer's proceeds (DT Marks DEFI LLC, per WLF's own disclosures) and on the passive investor roster of the bank now chartered to issue that token. Two documents, one architecture. No causal claim required.

Architecture II — Executive power

The Court's silence is the week's largest structural fact. Emergency application 26A124 — the administration's request to stay the injunctions against the March 31 mail-ballot executive order — has now been pending twenty days without any order, which election-law scholar Rick Hasen flags as possibly the longest the Court has ever sat on an emergency election motion A for the docket posture. While the Court sat, Judge Talwani issued a second, nationwide injunction on August 11 barring USPS from enforcing the EO's mail-ballot provisions for the November elections A — opinion PDF, D. Mass. ECF 183 — and both sides filed supplemental briefs August 12, the Solicitor General telling the Court “it is critical that the Court act promptly.” By inaction, the Court has so far let both injunctions stand. The administration is complying with them. Both facts matter.

The machinery, meanwhile, is being contested lawfully and losing — mostly. DOJ is now roughly 0-for-20 in suits demanding state voter rolls; the Sixth Circuit denied en banc rehearing in the Michigan case August 14, though with several Republican-appointed judges writing in partial sympathy — the tally worth watching is appellate judges, not district losses B. The Fifth Circuit went the other way on August 13, reinstating nine provisions of Texas SB1 (mail-ballot ID matching among them) for November B. DOJ election monitors deployed to Minnesota's primary August 11 — the department's own release says more than fifty monitors across seven counties this season and that monitoring “continues through the general election on November 3, 2026” A. Twenty-six states are suing over DHS/FEMA's conditioning of ~$1B in homeland-security grants on election-procedure changes; no ruling yet A for the complaint. In Missouri, the Secretary of State blocked a referendum on the new congressional map from the ballot on certification day without disclosing signature counts (“Whether they had one signature or 500 million signatures doesn't really matter”); the suit is at hearing August 18, with a September 8 ballot-printing deadline B.

The trial balloon. I'll leave it at that.” B, video anchored — and note the standing February denial that any election-emergency EO draft existed was scoped to knowledge of the draft, which this remark neither repeats nor reaffirms. On 2028, the same night, the tempo ran the other direction: “I'd love to run, but the law is very strong” — twice, on camera A. That is a more 22nd-Amendment-deferential formulation than anything since spring; log the oscillation, assert nothing.
